I think in systems —
and build the ones that don't exist yet.

Driven by curiosity, grounded in systems thinking — I see complexity and reach for clarity. I lead with taste for what's worth building, the agency to build it myself rather than wait for it, and the discipline to leave out everything that isn't essential.

When I joined Meta, I had never written a line of production code. I'd spent my career building at scale — global policy coalitions, research programs, institutional partnerships — but always at arm's length from the actual product. I was good at it. But I kept hitting the same wall: the systems my programs needed didn't exist, and requesting them meant waiting months in engineering queues for tools that might never get prioritized.

So I decided to build them myself.

The Technical Leap

In 12 weeks, I went from zero engineering experience to shipping 100+ production code changes on my first internal tool—with no engineering support. I eventually built six production systems in total. I taught myself by doing: starting with small automations, breaking things, reading documentation, asking engineers the right questions, and shipping incrementally. I didn't wait for permission or formal training. I saw what was needed and figured out how to make it exist.

What surprised me most: the hardest part wasn't learning to code. It was learning to think in systems—understanding how data flows between tools, how to design for edge cases, how to build something that other people (not just me) can maintain and extend. That's the skill that transfers everywhere.

The Through-Line

My career spans the World Economic Forum, the Aspen Institute, University of San Francisco, and Meta—all different contexts, same instinct: I see what's missing and I build toward it. At the WEF, that meant building a 150+ member global community and overseeing the working groups that produced landmark whitepapers on blockchain's environmental impact. At Aspen, it meant building an MVP for a health data privacy system and presenting findings to the California Privacy Protection Agency and the Electronic Frontier Foundation. At Meta, it meant teaching myself to code and shipping the production tools a 1,000+ person organization now runs on.

My goal isn't to become a full-time software engineer. It's to be the person who sees the gap between what an organization needs and what exists—and closes it. Sometimes that means writing code. Sometimes it means designing a system. Sometimes it means changing a process. The common thread is: I build things that actually get used — and that actually matter.

What I'm building toward

I'm most alive when the path isn't clear and nobody's waiting for permission to start. That's been true across every role — from building global policy coalitions at the World Economic Forum to shipping production code at Meta without an engineering team.

I want to build things people actually reach for — features that land in the real world, tools that change how things get done at scale. And I've done exactly that: deploying LLM agents and agentic workflows in production, shipping AI-native systems that real teams depend on. The experience of building with AI — not just using it, but designing the seams between what it handles and where human judgment has to hold the line — is where the most interesting questions live. I want to keep working there.

I've moved across policy, research, operations, and engineering — not because I couldn't go deep, but because the problems worth solving rarely sit inside one domain. Different maps, same compass.

What I Believe

01
Technical problems are almost always downstream of organizational ones. The hardest part of building an AI routing system wasn't the AI. It was mapping which internal tool owned which problem — and where those ownerships overlapped. Nobody had that map. Once the taxonomy existed, the routing logic took hours. The prerequisite wasn't technical; it was organizational. I've since seen this pattern everywhere: when a system isn't working, the bottleneck is almost never where it appears to be.
02
Strong judgment means knowing what to leave out. The PM with conviction about what NOT to build is rarer than the one who wants to add more. This is especially true in AI-native systems — where AI should handle something and where human judgment has to hold the line isn't a technical question, it's a judgment call. Getting it wrong costs more than time. The ability to align people around a difficult "no" is one of the most underrated things a PM can do.
03
Credibility compounds. Tools that are accurate and on time attract institutional weight they weren't designed for — teams start planning around them, VPs reference them in reviews, partner teams build on top of them. None of that gets designed in. It accrues because there's always scarcity of things people can actually trust. The most durable product moat is reliability.
04
The gap between insight and impact used to feel fixed. It isn't anymore. I've always been the person who saw a broken process and started sketching a fix before anyone asked. Zero-to-one work — building something from nothing — is where I've always felt most alive. What AI changed isn't that instinct; it's the scale of what I can reach for. Problems I once had to accept as engineering backlog, or simply work around, I can now just solve. The creative surface has expanded completely. And honestly, I've had more fun building these past few years than at any other point in my career — not just shipping, but the process of figuring it out, the resourcefulness of it, the feeling of making something real that didn't exist before. That kind of energy doesn't go away. I'm genuinely excited about what comes next and whatever role I get to play in building it.
