iWAM Admin Dashboard

Compliance Analytics Module — Built into Production iWAM

A new admin analytics layer built directly into Meta's internal regulatory management system — replacing manual monthly data calculations with real-time obligation tracking, regional breakdowns, deadline risk indicators, and one-click HPM report generation.

Type New feature in production React app
Production Diffs 23–32
Access Gated — admin permissions only
Status Launched & live

⚠ The Problem

iWAM — Meta's internal system for tracking integrity obligations and requirements — held all the data the program needed. Obligation counts, requirements status, regional breakdowns, intake velocity, completion rates across issue areas like child and youth safety, fraud and scams, harmful and illegal content, and novel technologies. It was all in there. The problem was that it existed at the row level: each obligation was a record, each requirement was a record, and there was no aggregate view that showed how the program was actually doing.

Getting a useful picture required analysis, not just extraction. Every month I'd pull the raw data and do the work myself: aggregate obligation counts by region and priority, calculate intake velocity over the last month, half, and year, identify which risk areas were accelerating or stalling, and map patterns across all of those dimensions simultaneously to produce something a VP could actually act on. Were fraud and scams obligations coming in faster this half than last? Was APAC intake slowing or accelerating? Was child safety completion falling behind relative to intake? None of those questions had pre-computed answers — I had to derive them. This took hours, and produced a static snapshot that started aging the moment it was finished.

That monthly calculation powered the HPMs (the monthly Happenings, People & Moments program health reports), but it also powered everything else: ad-hoc leadership requests, standing cross-functional briefings with partner teams who needed regular program status updates, and any moment a VP wanted a number before a meeting. Every ask required running the same analysis from scratch. I was the single point of access for data that already existed in a system everyone used — which meant that every time someone needed an answer, they had to wait for me.

⚙ What I Built

Rather than proposing a new standalone tool, I built the analytics layer directly inside iWAM — organizing and surfacing data that was already in the system but had no consolidated view. The dashboard is gated behind admin permissions and lives within the existing production React codebase.

Tier 1 — Always Visible:

  • Half-year selector for filtering all metrics by execution deadline period (H1/H2)
  • Auto-generated TLDR banner with live obligation and requirement counts, P0 breakdown, and deadline risk summary
  • "Copy as HPM Summary" button — one click formats all dashboard metrics into structured text ready for the monthly Happenings, People & Moments health report (HPM), replacing hours of manual formatting
  • In Focus priority table: P0/P1/P2 rows showing obligation counts, completed vs. pending, with clickable deep-links to filtered detail views
  • Intake Volume tracking: new obligations entering the pipeline over time, by week, month, half, and year

Tier 2 — Tabbed Deep-Dives:

  • Regional Breakdown: APAC, EMEA, NORAM, LATAM obligation tables with drill-down into requirements and completion status
  • RRC Flow: regulatory readiness completion scores by track, surfacing which areas are behind on documentation
  • Intake Trends: month-over-month volume charts showing obligation intake patterns by region and issue area
  • Deadline risk indicators: color-coded by urgency — overdue, within 14 days, within 30 days, on track
  • Narrative analysis paragraphs auto-generated from live data, ready to copy directly into leadership updates

✦ What I Learned

Most product instinct points toward building something new when something isn't working. The more calibrated move is to figure out whether the problem is the tool itself or just what it's showing you — and whether fixing that actually requires building anything new at all. iWAM already had the data. It just couldn't surface it in a useful format. Building an admin view inside iWAM rather than proposing a separate analytics product meant the dashboard inherited the system's trust, permissions, and data connections on day one. Adoption was immediate; there was no change management to run, no migration to manage, no convincing people to use a new thing. Shreyas Doshi (ex-Director of Product at Stripe, ex-Group PM at Google) frames this as identifying where the actual constraint is before reaching for a solution — a lot of product decisions go wrong because the problem gets diagnosed one layer too late.

The other thing that only became clear after the fact: when one person is the single point of access for a key data set, that can feel like competence from the inside. I was fast. I was reliable. But that reliability was its own bottleneck — colleagues had to wait for me, VPs had to ask, partner teams had to schedule time. When the dashboard removed that dependency, the benefit wasn't just my recovered hours. It was the friction that disappeared for everyone who needed the information and hadn't been asking because asking cost effort. Shishir Mehrotra (co-founder and CEO of Coda, ex-VP of Product at YouTube) talks about this as reducing the "tax" on the people around a tool — the biggest tax this removed wasn't mine.

What surprised me most was how quickly informal use became institutional dependency. The dashboard was built for my team's workflow. Within a few months it was being pulled up in VP-level program reviews, referenced by partner teams in their own cross-functional briefings, and depended on by colleagues when I was unavailable. I didn't design it for that scale. But tools that are accurate and reliable at their core tend to attract institutional weight organically — because there's always a scarcity of things people can actually trust. Credibility compounds.
