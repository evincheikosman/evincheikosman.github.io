IRC People & Process Web Page

Integrity, Risk & Compliance — Self-Service Operational Platform

Every program has a People & Process doc. I turned ours into a live internal website — restructuring the program's entire operational knowledge base into searchable, role-based navigation that anyone could use without asking for help.

Replaced 15+ tab Google Doc
Status Launched & live
Engineering Support None
Roadmapping Module Launched & live

⚠ The Problem

Operational knowledge for a complex compliance program—team structure, escalation processes, role definitions, meeting cadences, communication channels, and process documentation—lived in a sprawling multi-tab document. It was difficult to navigate, impossible to search, and perpetually out of date.

New team members spent days just figuring out how the program worked. Existing members couldn't quickly reference a process without scrolling through dozens of tabs. Every question required asking a person instead of finding the answer independently.

⚙ What I Built

I rebuilt the entire operational knowledge base as a live internal website—structured, searchable, and designed for self-service. The core design principle: organize information around how people actually look for it (by role, by process, by situation), not around how it was originally written.

  • Structured navigation by team role, process type, and use case
  • Quick-reference guides for the most common workflows
  • Contact directories and escalation path documentation
  • Meeting cadences and communication channel maps
  • Step-by-step process documentation with clear ownership
  • Single canonical URL — one place, always current

→ Into a Delivery Hub

Once the reference site was stable, I extended it into an operational delivery platform by adding a dedicated roadmapping module. Roadmapping is done ahead of every half to give product teams visibility into which regulatory obligations are coming — so they can allocate resources, scope the required work (new feature, product change, or net-new build), and prepare before deadlines arrive rather than after. Previously this coordination lived across spreadsheets and iWAM records, communicated through Workplace posts that got buried. There was no persistent home for the roadmap.

The module didn't replace the Workplace posts — those still went out when each half was finalized. What it replaced was the durability problem. Rather than living only in a post that scrolls out of view, the roadmap now had a permanent, navigable home: current obligations, prior halves, track impact, product surfaces in scope, and deadlines, organized by region and always accessible. The post communicates; the module is where the plan lives.

The workflow improvement was significant. What previously required hours of cross-referencing spreadsheets, pulling from iWAM, and manually formatting for a post could now be entered directly into the module. Plug in the obligations, regions, tracks, and deadlines — the structured view populates in seconds.

✦ What I Learned

The 15-tab Google Doc wasn't broken because nobody cared — it was organized around how the content was created, not how people needed to use it. Rebuilding it meant starting from user intent rather than content origin: what does someone on the legal team need at 9am before a meeting? What does a new hire need on day one? What does an ops lead need when a process changes? The answers made the structure obvious. That's an information architecture problem, and mapping access patterns before designing structure is one of the most underrated disciplines in product and program work. Most internal tooling fails not because it's missing features, but because nobody thought clearly about who opens it, when, and what question they're trying to answer.

The roadmapping module taught me something related: there's a meaningful difference between communication and reference, and most teams conflate them. A Workplace post is good for announcing something — it's a bad place to keep something. Once I understood that the problem wasn't the post itself, but that posts were being used as the only home for information that needed to persist, the solution was obvious. The post communicates the roadmap; the module is where the roadmap lives. Keeping those two functions separate, and designing for each deliberately, is a pattern that applies well beyond this project. Most information architecture failures happen because something that should be a reference ends up only as a communication — and once the post is buried, the knowledge is gone with it.
