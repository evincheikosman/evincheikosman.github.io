Monthly Program Health Reports (HPMs)

Happenings, People & Moments

A monthly program health report — five consecutive editions, zero missed months — that became the org's primary accountability mechanism for 2,000+ regulatory requirements across 10+ jurisdictions.

⚠ The Problem

A regulatory compliance portfolio of 2,000+ requirements across 10+ jurisdictions needed regular health monitoring. The data lived in iWAM — Meta's internal system for tracking integrity obligations and requirements — but it existed at the row level. Each obligation was a record, each requirement was a record, and there was no way to look across all of it and understand what the program was actually doing.

Getting a useful picture required analysis, not just extraction. Every month I'd pull the raw data and do the work myself: aggregate obligation counts by region and priority, calculate intake velocity, identify which risk areas — child and youth safety, fraud and scams, harmful and illegal content, novel technologies — were accelerating or stalling, and map patterns across all of those dimensions simultaneously to produce something a VP could actually act on. Leadership had no reliable way to ask: where do we stand? What's been completed this month? Is the program accelerating or stalling? Those questions required someone to sit down and derive the answers — and that someone was me.

⚙ What I Built

A monthly program health report — called the HPM (Happenings, People & Moments) — with a consistent structure, published every month without exception. Each edition covers:

  • Portfolio summary — total obligations, requirements, and P0 breakdown
  • Execution progress — what was completed this month
  • On-track status for top priority obligations with percentage readiness
  • Requirement growth analysis — how and why the portfolio is expanding
  • Legal review pipeline — backlog status and throughput trends
  • Velocity metrics — requirements cleared per month, trend over time
  • Track grades — performance across each integrity work stream
  • Resources and contacts — People & Process, IRC Escalation Hub, RITE, iWAM
  • Deep-dives — ad-hoc analysis on high-risk obligations and upcoming enforcement deadlines

◎ Reach & Distribution

Each edition was published as a pinned Workplace post, tagged to VP leads, legal and policy POCs, product program managers, and surface owners across all regions. VP-level stakeholders referenced it in leadership reviews; partner teams used it for their own cross-functional briefings. The structure — Happenings, People, and Moments — was designed to be scannable in under a minute.

The data powering each edition was pulled manually from iWAM and cross-referenced across spreadsheets and track updates until I built the iWAM Admin Dashboard (Project 06) to automate that layer. The HPM came first; the dashboard was built to serve it.

✦ What I Learned

Consistent delivery is its own product. Once the HPM was publishing on a reliable monthly cadence — five editions, five months, no gaps — teams started planning their execution cycles around it — completing requirements before the report dropped, not after, because they knew it would surface publicly. That's a behavioral shift. But the content itself also mattered independently: the HPM synthesized 2,000+ requirements worth of iWAM data into three or four program-level insights that VPs could cite in leadership reviews and partner teams could reference in their own cross-functional briefings. The raw data was available to anyone who wanted to pull it from iWAM. What wasn't available was the interpretation — what the numbers meant in aggregate, what was accelerating and what was stalling, and what it implied about the program's trajectory. That synthesis was what people actually depended on. The cadence made them expect it. The content quality made them trust it.

The hardest editorial decision was what to do when the data implicated a partner team. When the legal review backlog appeared in the numbers, publishing the raw count — "47 of 412 reviews completed this cycle" — would have signaled to the org that legal was the bottleneck, which was true but incomplete. The actual constraint was bandwidth, not effort or priority. Learning to write "continued throughput is needed to clear the backlog ahead of H2 deadlines" instead of leading with the gap kept the report trusted by all sides. Maintaining accountability without assigning blame is a distinct skill from data analysis, and it's the one that determined whether people actually read the next edition.

Data without interpretation is just reporting. The requirement count was rising every month — which could reasonably read as the program falling behind. The real story was that the regulatory environment was expanding and the program was keeping pace. Writing that distinction into every edition required understanding the legislative context, not just the metrics. The most valuable thing the HPM did was give leadership a coherent narrative, not a spreadsheet.
